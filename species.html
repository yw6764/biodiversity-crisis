The species behind the supply chain

The orangutan is not disappearing all at once.

Forest by forest, orangutans are being pushed into smaller and more fragmented habitats. Their decline begins long before extinction becomes final.

Why begin with orangutans?

Orangutans are familiar enough to invite care, but specific enough to keep this project focused. Their story brings biodiversity loss down to a scale that can be seen: one species, two islands, and a shrinking rainforest habitat connected to global demand for palm oil.

The IUCN Red List classifies both the Bornean orangutan and the Sumatran orangutan as Critically Endangered.[1] [2] This status is not just a warning about the future. It describes a crisis already underway: declining populations, fragmented habitats, and growing pressure from land-use change.

Two critically endangered species

Borneo

Bornean Orangutan

Pongo pygmaeus

  • Status: Critically Endangered
  • Population trend: Decreasing
  • Main threat: Habitat loss and fragmentation
  • Key pressures: forest conversion, agriculture, plantations, and roads

The Bornean orangutan shows how habitat loss changes more than the amount of land available. When forests are split apart, orangutans also lose travel routes, nesting areas, food sources, and safer distance from human activity.[1]

Sumatra

Sumatran Orangutan

Pongo abeliili>

  • Status: Critically Endangered
  • Population trend: Decreasing
  • Main threat: Habitat loss from logging, agriculture, and oil palm plantations
  • Key pressures: roads, forest conversion, and human–orangutan conflict

For the Sumatran orangutan, palm oil is not an abstract issue. Oil palm plantations and roads directly change the forests where the species lives, feeds, and moves. The result is a smaller, more divided landscape for a species that depends on connected forest.[2]

Extinction begins before a species disappears.

Extinction is often imagined as one final moment: a species exists, and then it does not. But biodiversity loss usually begins much earlier. Ceballos, Ehrlich, and Dirzo argue that the current extinction crisis is also marked by population losses, local disappearances, and shrinking geographic ranges.[3]

This matters for orangutans because a species can still be alive while its future is being narrowed. When populations become smaller and more isolated, survival becomes more fragile. The crisis is not only the loss of individual animals, but the loss of the conditions that allow the species to continue.

Key idea

A species can still exist and still be losing its future. Population decline, shrinking habitat, and isolation are already forms of disappearance.

The forest disappears piece by piece.

Habitat loss is rarely experienced as one dramatic event. It happens through clearing, roads, plantations, and repeated decisions that may look small on their own. Over time, these changes reshape the forest into a landscape that can no longer support the same life.

That is why orangutan decline is also a story about time. The damage becomes visible slowly: fewer connected forests, fewer safe routes, fewer places to feed and nest. By the time the loss is obvious, many of the relationships that sustained the species have already been broken.

From species to system

An orangutan is not only an animal in danger. It is a clue.

Its decline points outward: to forests, plantations, roads, commodity chains, consumer goods, and public attention. The next page turns from the animal to the landscape, showing how Indonesian forest loss makes gradual environmental damage visible.
